Lawrence Hill's literary short story, "So, What Are You, Anyway?", serves as a profound allegory exploring the complexity of racial identity, the intrusiveness of adult social constructs, and the resilience of childhood innocence. The narrative follows Carole, a young mixed-race girl traveling unaccompanied on an airplane, who finds herself subjected to invasive interrogation by a white couple seated beside her, the Nortons.

Through subtle conversational microaggressions, the Nortons struggle to categorize Carole within their rigid binary understanding of race. Carole's confusion stems not from a lack of self-awareness, but from her refusal to view herself through an essentialist racial lens. When pressured to state what color her mother and father are, Carole describes them using relatable childhood imagery—comparing her parents to familiar everyday colors and doll figures rather than arbitrary social classifications.

### 1. The Burden of Racial Categorization

The story illustrates how adult social structures impose racial categorizations onto children who have not yet internalized societal prejudices. The Nortons' obsession with determining Carole's racial composition reveals a broader societal discomfort with racial ambiguity and multiracial identities.

### 2. Family Modeling and Empathetic Resistance

Carole's internal recollections of her parents provide an emotional anchor against the Nortons' probing questions. Her father's advice to treat others with dignity and her mother's affirmation of her individual worth enable Carole to maintain her composure, even when confronted with veiled hostility.

### 3. Conclusion

Ultimately, Lawrence Hill's work challenges readers to examine how casual interpersonal interactions can perpetuate systemic prejudice. By presenting the narrative through Carole's innocent perspective, the story exposes the absurdity of racial prejudice and affirms the necessity of creating empathetic, inclusive communities.
